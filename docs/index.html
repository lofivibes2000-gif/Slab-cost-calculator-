Quick answers

The three questions everyone asks first

How many bags of concrete do I need?

Divide the pour's volume in cubic feet by the bag yield, then round up: 0.30 ft³ for a 40 lb bag, 0.375 ft³ for 50 lb, 0.45 ft³ for 60 lb, 0.60 ft³ for 80 lb. A 10 × 10 ft slab at 4 inches is 1.23 yd³ — about 62 bags of 80 lb with 10% waste.

How many yards of concrete do I need?

Length × width × thickness (in feet) ÷ 27. Or use the shortcut: (square feet × thickness in inches) ÷ 324. A 20 × 20 ft slab at 4 inches is (400 × 4) ÷ 324 = 4.94 cubic yards. Order about 5.5 with waste.

How much does a concrete slab cost?

A standard residential slab runs $6–$12 per square foot installed in 2026. Concrete material alone is about $2–$3.30 per sq ft — labor is usually the biggest line. A 20 × 20 slab with base, forms, mesh, labor, joints and curing lands around $3,200–$4,400 depending on scope.

What a concrete slab cost actually includes

Most online calculators hand you a material number and call it a cost. A real slab is an assembly of a dozen line items, and skipping any one of them is how a $3,000 quote becomes a $7,000 invoice. Here is the full list the calculator prices.

Concrete material

Ready-mix priced per cubic yard. One cubic yard covers 81 sq ft at 4 inches, and each 500 PSI step up adds $4–$10 per yard. Usually 20–30% of the total.

Delivery & fees

$80–$180 per load, plus a $50–$200 short-load fee on orders under 4 to 5 yards, plus fuel and environmental surcharges. A single yard delivered runs $195–$295.

Site prep & excavation

Grading, removing topsoil, establishing drainage slope, and compacting. Poor soil or significant excavation pushes this to $2.50 per sq ft. Usually 10–15% of the total.

Gravel sub-base

4 inches of compacted crushed stone under residential slabs, 6 inches under driveways. $0.75–$1.90 per sq ft. Skipping base is the number one cause of early cracking and heave.

Forms & edge setup

$1.50–$3.00 per linear foot of perimeter, including lumber, stakes, squaring up, and setting the slope for drainage. Stepped or curved forms cost more.

Reinforcement

Fiber $0.10–$0.32, wire mesh $0.20–$0.90, rebar grid $0.70–$2.00 per sq ft. It must be supported at mid-depth — steel lying on the sub-base does nothing.

Placing & finishing labor

$3–$8 per sq ft — the single biggest line, usually 45–55% of the total. A crew of 3–5 spends 40–70 labor hours on a 1,000 sq ft slab.

Control joints

Saw-cut every 2–3× the slab thickness in feet, capped at 15 ft. $1.60–$3.60 per linear foot, with a $150–$225 minimum. This is what stops random cracking.

Curing & sealing

Curing compound applied as soon as the surface water is gone, plus an optional sealer. $0.25–$1.00 per sq ft. Concrete that dries out too early loses surface strength.

The single biggest takeaway: the concrete itself is only about a quarter of an installed slab. Labor is usually half. That is why a materials-only estimate of $2–$4.50 per sq ft is not a cheaper slab — it is a slab with no crew, no base, and no forms.

Where to save

Cut the cost without cutting corners

There is a meaningful gap between a well-scoped slab and an over-specified one. These are the moves that reduce the bill without risking the result:

  • Get the base right, not bigger. A 4-inch compacted base is the residential standard. Going to 6 inches adds $0.60–$0.90 per sq ft and buys almost nothing on decent soil. What matters is that the base is compacted and uniform — not its depth.
  • Match reinforcement to the load. Wire mesh on a patio is enough; a driveway needs #3 rebar at 18 inches on center. Putting #4 at 12 inches on a walkway is paying for capacity you will never use.
  • Keep the pour accessible. If the truck can reach the forms, you avoid a $450–$900 pump truck. Moving the access point or using a wider gate is free by comparison.
  • Pour bigger pads, not more pads. Each additional pour means another truck minimum, another crew mobilization, and another set of forms. Two 10 × 10 pads cost more than one 10 × 20 pad.
  • Order one truckload. A 10-yard truck costs $1,450–$1,950 delivered. Two 5-yard loads cost more, because the trip fee is charged twice.
  • Finish during the pour. Adding a decorative finish later costs more than specifying a broom or trowel finish on the original pour.
Where not to save

The three places to never cut corners

These are the items where a cheap quote turns into a failure — and where the repair costs far more than the original saving.

1. Site preparation and compaction

A slab is only as good as what is under it. Skipping the base or pouring over uncompacted fill is the number one cause of early cracking and heave. A 4-inch compacted base runs $0.75–$1.90 per sq ft, and it is the cheapest insurance in the whole project. On poor soil, engineering fill can push this to $2.50 per sq ft — and skipping it costs more later.

2. Concrete strength and air entrainment

In any freeze-thaw climate, specify 4,000 PSI with 5–7% air entrainment per ACI 332. At 3,000 PSI without air, exterior slabs scale and spall — sometimes within 5 to 10 years. Each 500 PSI step up costs only $4–$10 per cubic yard. It is the cheapest upgrade on the page.

3. Control joints and curing

Concrete cracks. Control joints decide where it cracks, in a straight line, instead of in a random diagonal across your patio. Joints cost $1.60–$3.60 per linear foot with a $150 minimum, and curing compound adds $0.25–$1.00 per sq ft. Together they are well under 10% of the budget and they are what keeps a slab looking intentional for 30 years.

A quote under $4 per square foot is missing something. On a residential flatwork scope, $4 per sq ft cannot cover concrete, a crew, forms, base, reinforcement, joints, and curing. Find out what was left out before you sign it.

How much does a concrete slab cost per square foot?

A standard residential concrete slab runs $6 to $12 per square foot installed in 2026, with most projects landing near $8 per square foot. That figure includes ready-mix concrete, delivery, a compacted gravel base, forms, reinforcement, placing and finishing labor, control joints, and curing compound — but not major excavation, permits, or decorative finishes.

The cost per square foot breaks down roughly like this:

  • Concrete material: $2–$3.30 per sq ft
  • Labor (pour and finish): $3–$8 per sq ft — usually the single biggest line
  • Site prep and forms: $1–$2.50 per sq ft
  • Reinforcement: $0.20–$2.00 per sq ft
  • Curing and sealer: $0.25–$1.00 per sq ft

Small slabs cost more per square foot than large ones because the truck delivery, crew minimum, and forms are fixed costs spread over less area. A 10 x 10 pad typically runs $8.50–$15.00 per sq ft, while a 40 x 60 driveway lands closer to $6.50–$12.50 per sq ft. Decorative work — stamped, colored, or exposed aggregate — adds $3 to $10 per sq ft on top.

One important caveat about scope. Published per-square-foot figures disagree by nearly 40%, and the reason is that they measure different things. A quote at $6 per sq ft is often scoped to exclude delivery fees, control joints, curing compound, and site prep — real costs that a complete project pays. Price the same scope before comparing two bids; a lower number is not a better deal if it is missing items.

How much does it cost to pour a concrete slab?

Pouring a concrete slab costs $4 to $14 per square foot installed, depending on thickness, reinforcement, finish, and how much site work the ground needs. A plain broom-finished 4-inch slab on a flat, accessible site sits at the low end; a thick, reinforced slab on ground that needs grading and fill sits at the high end.

Realistic totals for common sizes at $6–$12 per sq ft:

  • 10 x 10 pad (100 sq ft): $600–$1,200
  • 12 x 12 pad (144 sq ft): $864–$1,730
  • 20 x 20 slab (400 sq ft): $2,400–$4,800
  • 24 x 24 garage pad (576 sq ft): $3,500–$9,000
  • 30 x 30 slab (900 sq ft): $3,600–$7,200

A useful shortcut: the concrete itself is only about 25% of an installed slab. Labor is usually 45–55%. That is why a slab quoted at $4 per square foot for materials alone is not out of line — it simply excludes the crew.

How many bags of concrete do I need?

Divide the pour's volume in cubic feet by the finished yield of one bag, then round up. Standard yields are 0.30 ft³ for a 40 lb bag, 0.45 ft³ for a 60 lb bag, and 0.60 ft³ for an 80 lb bag.

Bags = ceil( volume in ft³ x (1 + waste%) / yield per bag )

One cubic yard of concrete equals about 90 bags of 40 lb, 60 bags of 60 lb, or 45 bags of 80 lb mix.

Worked example — a 10 x 10 ft slab at 4 inches thick:

  • Volume = 10 x 10 x (4 ÷ 12) = 33.3 ft³ = 1.23 yd³
  • With 10% waste: 36.7 ft³
  • 80 lb bags: ceil(36.7 ÷ 0.60) = 62 bags
  • 60 lb bags: ceil(36.7 ÷ 0.45) = 82 bags

Bagged mix stops making sense above roughly 1 cubic yard. Past about 45 bags of 80 lb mix, hauling, mixing, and pouring by hand costs more in time and labor than a ready-mix truck — and cold joints between batches are far more likely.

How many yards of concrete do I need?

Multiply length by width by thickness in feet, then divide by 27. There are 27 cubic feet in a cubic yard.

Cubic yards = (Length ft x Width ft x Thickness in ÷ 12) ÷ 27
Shortcut: Cubic yards = (sq ft x thickness in) ÷ 324

For a 20 x 20 ft slab at 4 inches: (400 x 4) ÷ 324 = 4.94 cubic yards. Add 5–10% for waste and order about 5.5 yards.

As a rule of thumb, one cubic yard covers about 81 sq ft at 4 inches, 65 sq ft at 5 inches, 54 sq ft at 6 inches, and 41 sq ft at 8 inches. A standard ready-mix truck carries about 10 cubic yards.

How thick should a concrete slab be?

Spec it by load, not by rule of thumb:

  • 3.5–4 in — walkways, garden paths, patios, shed and AC pads (foot traffic only)
  • 4 in — the standard residential minimum; patios and light floors
  • 5 in — residential driveways and garage floors carrying passenger cars or half-ton pickups
  • 6 in — driveways taking trucks and RVs, heavy equipment pads, and commercial slabs
  • 8 in — post-tensioned, heavily loaded, or industrial slabs

For code: the International Residential Code (R506.1) sets a 3.5-inch minimum for slab-on-grade floors in habitable space, and most jurisdictions amend that to 4 inches. The IRC does not regulate exterior patios and walkways, though local codes often do govern driveways — published driveway standards run from 4 to 7 inches depending on the municipality.

ACI 330R-08 puts it plainly: "As a practical matter, there is no benefit in building slabs less than 4 in. (100 mm) thick." Thickness is the cheapest structural upgrade available — bending capacity scales with the square of thickness, so 4 in to 6 in is 50% more concrete but roughly 2.25x the bending resistance.

What PSI concrete do I need for a slab?

Match the strength to the exposure and the load:

  • 3,000 PSI — interior slabs, basement floors, and shop floors that never see salt or freezing
  • 3,500 PSI — standard patios, sidewalks, and exterior residential work
  • 4,000 PSI — driveways, garage floors, and any exterior slab in a freeze-thaw climate, specified with 5–7% air entrainment
  • 4,500–5,000 PSI — heavy vehicle pads, warehouse slabs, and structural work per ACI 318

ACI 332 requires 4,000 PSI with air entrainment for exterior residential slabs in freeze-thaw zones. Each 500 PSI step up adds roughly $4–$10 per cubic yard, which is cheap insurance against scaling and spalling. In freeze-thaw climates, air entrainment matters more than an extra inch of thickness — 3,000 PSI slabs without air can scale badly within 5 to 10 years.

How do I order concrete for my slab?

You order by the cubic yard, not by the bag, when you use ready-mix.

  1. Compute your order quantity including the waste allowance — the calculator does this for you.
  2. Round up to the next quarter yard, which is how plants sell it.
  3. Call the plant and ask for the delivered price at your yardage, including any short-load fee, fuel surcharge, and environmental fee.
  4. Specify the PSI, air entrainment if you are in a freeze-thaw climate, and the aggregate size.
  5. Confirm the delivery window and whether a pump is required.
  6. Have forms, base, reinforcement, and a crew ready before the truck arrives — a ready-mix truck waits, and waiting is billed.

For bagged concrete, buy the same product in one consistent mix and stage the bags so you do not run out mid-pour.

Do I need a gravel base under my concrete slab?

Yes. A compacted gravel base is the single most important thing under a slab, and skipping it is the number one cause of early cracking and heave.

4 inches of compacted crushed stone is the standard under residential slabs, and 6 inches is typical under driveways and garage floors. On a 1,000 sq ft slab, 4 inches of base works out to about 12.3 cubic yards — roughly 17 to 20 tons.

Installed base runs about $0.75 to $1.90 per square foot, depending on stone price and haul distance. On poor soil, engineered fill can push that to $2.50 per sq ft.

The base does three jobs: it distributes the load, it provides a stable uniform surface so the slab does not have to bridge over voids, and it lets water drain away instead of undermining the slab. A slab's strength depends more on the uniformity of what is underneath than on its own thickness.

How far apart should control joints be in a concrete slab?

Joint spacing should be no more than 2 to 3 times the slab thickness in inches. ACI 330R puts it at 30 times the thickness; the American Concrete Pavement Association uses 24 times with a 15-foot cap.

Practical spacing by thickness:

  • 4 in slab — joints every 8 to 10 ft
  • 5 in slab — joints every 10 to 12 ft
  • 6 in slab — joints every 12 to 15 ft
  • 8 in slab — joints every 15 ft (cap)

Control joints are the actual crack-control mechanism. Concrete cracks where you tell it to, and you tell it where the joint is. Reinforcement keeps cracks from opening wide, but the joint decides where the crack happens.

Saw-cut within a few hours of finishing, before the slab can cure enough to shrink. Tooled joints work for straight runs, but a saw gives you tighter control on curves and odd shapes.

Can I pour a concrete slab myself?

Yes, for small pours. A 10 x 10 patio at 4 inches is about 1.23 cubic yards — 62 bags of 80 lb mix, or one short ready-mix load.

What a DIY pour involves:

  1. Excavate and grade, then lay and compact the gravel base
  2. Install a vapor barrier and form the edges
  3. Set reinforcement on chairs at mid-depth
  4. Pour, screed, bull-float, edge, and broom or trowel finish
  5. Cut control joints, then cure and seal

The realistic DIY cost for materials only runs $2.50 to $4.50 per square foot for a 4-inch slab — roughly $250 to $450 for a 10 x 10 pad in bags. Add a mixer rental around $50–$90 per day if you are not batching by hand.

Where DIY gets expensive is labor, not material. A crew for a residential pour is 3 to 5 people, and 40 to 70 labor hours for 1,000 sq ft. For anything over about 2 cubic yards, professional placement usually costs less than doing it yourself.

Why does concrete cost more in my area than the national average?

Concrete pricing tracks local construction volume, fuel costs, and how many batch plants are competing for your order. The national average is a starting point, not a quote.

Regional ready-mix pricing in 2026:

  • Southeast (FL, GA, AL): $125–$145 per yd³
  • Midwest (OH, IN, IL): $135–$155 per yd³
  • Southwest (TX, AZ, NM): $140–$160 per yd³
  • Northeast (NY, MA, PA): $155–$180 per yd³
  • West Coast (CA, WA, OR): $165–$195 per yd³

Labor moves the installed price even more. The Northeast and West Coast add 15–35% to installed per-square-foot cost versus the Southeast and Midwest. Island and remote sites can push ready-mix past $250 per yard.

A useful slab cost calculator prices the whole installed job, not just the concrete. What is the total cost of a 20 x 20 concrete slab?

A 20 x 20 ft slab at 4 inches with a 4-inch gravel base, welded wire mesh and a broom finish costs $3,200 to $4,400 in 2026, with $4,417 as the typical figure — about $11.04 per square foot installed.

Here is where that $4,417 goes:

  • Ready-mix concrete (5.43 yd³ at $155/yd³) — $842
  • Delivery — $120
  • Site prep and excavation — $340
  • Gravel sub-base — $500
  • Forms and edge setup — $180
  • Reinforcement — $180
  • Vapor barrier — $80
  • Placing and finishing labor — $1,800
  • Control joints — $175
  • Curing compound and sealer — $200

The number moves fast: a 5-inch slab, a stamped finish or a pump truck will each add several hundred dollars.

Should I use the calculator's estimate or hire a contractor?

Use the estimate to plan and to check a quote — not as the final price. A calculator tells you what a job of this size should cost; a contractor tells you what it costs your job.

The estimate is strongest for a straightforward slab on accessible ground with no demolition and no tricky excavation. It is weakest when the site is tight, the access is bad, there is significant slope or poor soil, or the finish is decorative.

The practical use is arithmetic: when a contractor quotes $6,900 for a 20 x 20 slab, divide by 400 sq ft and you get $17.25 per square foot. Compare that against the $11.04 the model produces and you can see whether the gap is explained by scope — extra excavation, a thicker base, a pump truck, a stamped finish — or is simply margin.

Always get three quotes and compare them line by line, not just on the total.

Why do concrete slab costs vary so much between contractors?

Three things move the price: scope, access and geography.

Scope is the biggest lever. "$6 per square foot" usually means concrete and labor only — no base, no forms, no reinforcement, no joints, no curing compound, no excavation. Add those and the same slab is $11.

Access decides whether a truck can reach the pour. A short-load fee of $120–$200 applies when the load is under 10 yards, and a pump truck runs $450–$900. On a backyard with a 30-foot gate, those charges are real.

Geography moves ready-mix from $125/yd³ in the Southeast to $195/yd³ on the West Coast, and pushes installed labor 15–35% higher in the Northeast and West Coast than in the Southeast and Midwest.

Finally, a contractor buying concrete at volume gets a better rate than one placing a single slab — which is exactly why one-bag pricing and one-load pricing are the same thing.

How do I compare two concrete slab quotes fairly?

Put both quotes on the same line items before you compare totals. Most disagreements are scope differences dressed up as price differences.

Check for these seven lines on every quote: concrete (cubic yards and PSI), delivery and any short-load or pump charge, excavation and site prep, gravel base depth, forms and edge setup, reinforcement (size and spacing), labor, control joints, and curing compound or sealer.

Then verify the quantities. A 20 x 20 slab at 4 inches needs 5.43 cubic yards including waste, not 5. A 400 sq ft base at 4 inches needs about 1.48 cubic yards of gravel. If a quote is short on yards, it is short on concrete, and a short pour is a failed pour.

Two quotes that both include the same ten lines should land within about 15% of each other in a competitive market. If one is double, the gap is scope or margin, not price.

What materials do I need for a concrete slab?

A complete residential slab needs eight things, and most of them are forgotten until the day of the pour.

  • Concrete — 3,000 PSI for interior and sheltered work, 3,500 PSI for standard exterior residential, 4,000 PSI with air entrainment for driveways and freeze-thaw climates
  • Gravel base — 4 inches of compacted crushed stone (400 sq ft needs about 1.48 cubic yards)
  • Vapor barrier — 6 mil poly or 10 mil vapor retarder, laid over the base
  • Reinforcement — #4 rebar at 18 inches on center both ways, or 6 x 6 welded wire mesh
  • Forms — 2 x 4 lumber or rigid forms for the edges
  • Control joint tooling — a groover, or saw cuts within a few hours of finishing
  • Curing compound — applied once the surface hardens enough
  • Base and form stakes — and a plate compactor or roller
