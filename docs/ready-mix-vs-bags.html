Ready-mix or bags of concrete?

How much does a concrete slab cost per square foot?

A standard residential concrete slab runs $6 to $12 per square foot installed in 2026, with most projects landing near $8 per square foot. That figure includes ready-mix concrete, delivery, a compacted gravel base, forms, reinforcement, placing and finishing labor, control joints, and curing compound — but not major excavation, permits, or decorative finishes.

The cost per square foot breaks down roughly like this:

  • Concrete material: $2–$3.30 per sq ft
  • Labor (pour and finish): $3–$8 per sq ft — usually the single biggest line
  • Site prep and forms: $1–$2.50 per sq ft
  • Reinforcement: $0.20–$2.00 per sq ft
  • Curing and sealer: $0.25–$1.00 per sq ft

Small slabs cost more per square foot than large ones because the truck delivery, crew minimum, and forms are fixed costs spread over less area. A 10 x 10 pad typically runs $8.50–$15.00 per sq ft, while a 40 x 60 driveway lands closer to $6.50–$12.50 per sq ft. Decorative work — stamped, colored, or exposed aggregate — adds $3 to $10 per sq ft on top.

One important caveat about scope. Published per-square-foot figures disagree by nearly 40%, and the reason is that they measure different things. A quote at $6 per sq ft is often scoped to exclude delivery fees, control joints, curing compound, and site prep — real costs that a complete project pays. Price the same scope before comparing two bids; a lower number is not a better deal if it is missing items.

How much does it cost to pour a concrete slab?

Pouring a concrete slab costs $4 to $14 per square foot installed, depending on thickness, reinforcement, finish, and how much site work the ground needs. A plain broom-finished 4-inch slab on a flat, accessible site sits at the low end; a thick, reinforced slab on ground that needs grading and fill sits at the high end.

Realistic totals for common sizes at $6–$12 per sq ft:

  • 10 x 10 pad (100 sq ft): $600–$1,200
  • 12 x 12 pad (144 sq ft): $864–$1,730
  • 20 x 20 slab (400 sq ft): $2,400–$4,800
  • 24 x 24 garage pad (576 sq ft): $3,500–$9,000
  • 30 x 30 slab (900 sq ft): $3,600–$7,200

A useful shortcut: the concrete itself is only about 25% of an installed slab. Labor is usually 45–55%. That is why a slab quoted at $4 per square foot for materials alone is not out of line — it simply excludes the crew.

How many bags of concrete do I need?

Divide the pour's volume in cubic feet by the finished yield of one bag, then round up. Standard yields are 0.30 ft³ for a 40 lb bag, 0.45 ft³ for a 60 lb bag, and 0.60 ft³ for an 80 lb bag.

Bags = ceil( volume in ft³ x (1 + waste%) / yield per bag )

One cubic yard of concrete equals about 90 bags of 40 lb, 60 bags of 60 lb, or 45 bags of 80 lb mix.

Worked example — a 10 x 10 ft slab at 4 inches thick:

  • Volume = 10 x 10 x (4 ÷ 12) = 33.3 ft³ = 1.23 yd³
  • With 10% waste: 36.7 ft³
  • 80 lb bags: ceil(36.7 ÷ 0.60) = 62 bags
  • 60 lb bags: ceil(36.7 ÷ 0.45) = 82 bags

Bagged mix stops making sense above roughly 1 cubic yard. Past about 45 bags of 80 lb mix, hauling, mixing, and pouring by hand costs more in time and labor than a ready-mix truck — and cold joints between batches are far more likely.

How many yards of concrete do I need?

Multiply length by width by thickness in feet, then divide by 27. There are 27 cubic feet in a cubic yard.

Cubic yards = (Length ft x Width ft x Thickness in ÷ 12) ÷ 27
Shortcut: Cubic yards = (sq ft x thickness in) ÷ 324

For a 20 x 20 ft slab at 4 inches: (400 x 4) ÷ 324 = 4.94 cubic yards. Add 5–10% for waste and order about 5.5 yards.

As a rule of thumb, one cubic yard covers about 81 sq ft at 4 inches, 65 sq ft at 5 inches, 54 sq ft at 6 inches, and 41 sq ft at 8 inches. A standard ready-mix truck carries about 10 cubic yards.

How thick should a concrete slab be?

Spec it by load, not by rule of thumb:

  • 3.5–4 in — walkways, garden paths, patios, shed and AC pads (foot traffic only)
  • 4 in — the standard residential minimum; patios and light floors
  • 5 in — residential driveways and garage floors carrying passenger cars or half-ton pickups
  • 6 in — driveways taking trucks and RVs, heavy equipment pads, and commercial slabs
  • 8 in — post-tensioned, heavily loaded, or industrial slabs

For code: the International Residential Code (R506.1) sets a 3.5-inch minimum for slab-on-grade floors in habitable space, and most jurisdictions amend that to 4 inches. The IRC does not regulate exterior patios and walkways, though local codes often do govern driveways — published driveway standards run from 4 to 7 inches depending on the municipality.

ACI 330R-08 puts it plainly: "As a practical matter, there is no benefit in building slabs less than 4 in. (100 mm) thick." Thickness is the cheapest structural upgrade available — bending capacity scales with the square of thickness, so 4 in to 6 in is 50% more concrete but roughly 2.25x the bending resistance.

What PSI concrete do I need for a slab?

Match the strength to the exposure and the load:

  • 3,000 PSI — interior slabs, basement floors, and shop floors that never see salt or freezing
  • 3,500 PSI — standard patios, sidewalks, and exterior residential work
  • 4,000 PSI — driveways, garage floors, and any exterior slab in a freeze-thaw climate, specified with 5–7% air entrainment
  • 4,500–5,000 PSI — heavy vehicle pads, warehouse slabs, and structural work per ACI 318

ACI 332 requires 4,000 PSI with air entrainment for exterior residential slabs in freeze-thaw zones. Each 500 PSI step up adds roughly $4–$10 per cubic yard, which is cheap insurance against scaling and spalling. In freeze-thaw climates, air entrainment matters more than an extra inch of thickness — 3,000 PSI slabs without air can scale badly within 5 to 10 years.

Is it cheaper to buy ready-mix or bags of concrete?

For anything under 1 cubic yard, bags are usually cheaper overall. Above 1 cubic yard, ready-mix wins on both cost and effort.

The math on 1 cubic yard:

  • Ready-mix: $155/yd³ material, but a single delivered yard runs $195–$295 after trip charges and short-load fees
  • Bags: 45 bags of 80 lb at $6.50 = $292 in material — then you mix, haul, and pour 3,600 lb by hand

The material cost is nearly identical. The difference is labor and equipment, and that difference grows fast with volume. A ready-mix truck delivers, places, and screeds in minutes what takes several people half a day to do by hand.

The exception: a small pour in a backyard the truck cannot reach. If delivery means a pump truck or a long wheelbarrow haul, bags can still win on total cost even past a cubic yard.

What is the breakeven point between bags and ready-mix?

The breakeven sits around 1 cubic yard — 45 bags of 80 lb mix.

Below 1 yd³, bags cost less because you avoid a delivery trip fee, a short-load fee, and a minimum order. Between 1 and 2 yd³, ready-mix is roughly comparable on material but wins decisively on time. Above 2 yd³, ready-mix is the only sensible option — the labor savings alone are worth hundreds of dollars, and hand-mixing that volume reliably produces cold joints.

A practical rule of thumb:

  • Under 1 yd³ — bags. A 10 x 10 slab at 4 inches.
  • 1 to 2 yd³ — either works; compare the delivered quote against bags plus a mixer rental.
  • Over 2 yd³ — ready-mix, with a pump if the truck cannot reach the forms.

Do not forget the time cost. A one-cubic-yard hand mix is a full day of work for two people plus a mixer rental. A one-cubic-yard ready-mix pour is about an hour.

Can I mix concrete myself instead of using bags?

You can, and for medium-sized pours it is sometimes the best value. You need a drum mixer, Portland cement, sand, gravel, and water.

The classic 1:2:3 mix means 1 part cement, 2 parts sand, 3 parts aggregate by weight, which yields roughly 3,000 PSI after adding enough water to reach a workable consistency.

Three things decide whether hand-mixing works:

  1. Volume — mixing by hand is realistic to about 1 cubic yard per day with a proper machine. Beyond that you are building a production line.
  2. Consistency — every batch must get the same water. This is the hard part, and inconsistent water means inconsistent strength.
  3. Your back — one cubic yard of finished concrete weighs about 3,600 lb. That is a lot of lifting.

For anything under a cubic yard, bags are effectively hand-mixing with the mixing already done for you, and they are usually the better choice.

Should I use ready-mix or bags of concrete?

Use bags below about 1 cubic yard (roughly 45 bags of 80 lb mix). Use ready-mix above that.

Above a cubic yard, ready-mix is almost always cheaper per yard and far less work. At $155 per cubic yard delivered, a yard of concrete costs about $300 in material. The same yard in 80 lb bags at $6.50 each runs about $290 in bags — and then you still have to mix it, haul it, and pour it yourself, and a yard of finished concrete weighs about 3,600 lb.

The comparison changes once delivery fees enter. A single yard delivered costs $195–$295 after trip charges, and plants add a $50–$200 short-load fee below 4 to 5 yards. So for a small pour in a hard-to-reach backyard, bags can still win on total cost — they just take far longer.

The practical rule: bags for anything under 1 yd³, ready-mix from 1 to 2 yd³, and ready-mix with a pump beyond that if the truck cannot reach the forms.

Is it cheaper to buy ready-mix concrete or bags?

For a pour of 1 cubic yard or more, ready-mix is almost always cheaper once delivery is counted. Below that, bags win.

The material prices are close: a yard of ready-mix at $155 costs $155, while the same yard in 80 lb bags at $6.50 each (45 bags) costs $292. But a ready-mix order also carries $80 to $180 for delivery and a $60 to $200 short-load fee when the order is under 10 yards. A small ready-mix order can land near $400 all-in, which is still less than the bags once you count your time.

The real comparison is labor. One cubic yard of finished concrete weighs 4,050 lb. Hand-mixing that is a full day for two people plus a mixer rental.

So: bags below about 1 cubic yard, ready-mix above it — unless access is the deciding factor.

Should I use bags or ready-mix for a 10 x 10 slab?

A 10 x 10 ft slab at 4 inches is 1.23 cubic yards — right on the line where the decision is genuinely close.

With bags you need 62 bags of 80 lb mix (98 bags of 50 lb, 133 of 60 lb, 173 of 40 lb). At $6.50 per 80 lb bag that is about $403, plus a wheelbarrow, a mixing tub, and about five to six hours of work for two people across a weekend.

With ready-mix you order 1.5 cubic yards with waste. That is roughly $232 in concrete plus $120 delivery, plus a $60 short-load fee because it is under 10 yards — call it $410 delivered to the curb, and the crew has it placed in under an hour.

The money is a wash. The decision is time and access: if a mixer truck can reach the pour, buy ready-mix; if it cannot get through a gate, bags win because the truck's short-load and access charges will exceed the difference.

Should I use bags or ready-mix for a 20 x 20 slab?

Ready-mix, without question. A 20 x 20 ft slab at 4 inches is 5.43 cubic yards — and that is 245 bags of 80 lb mix.

At $6.50 a bag, 245 bags is $1,593, and you would be mixing roughly 20,000 lb of concrete by hand and wheelbarrowing it to the pour. Realistically that is two full days for two people plus a rental mixer, and bagged concrete has a limited working time once mixed.

Ready-mix for the same slab is about $842 in concrete and $120 for delivery — one truck load, placed in well under an hour.

The gap is not close. The crossover point is around 1 cubic yard: below that, bags save money and time; above it, a truck is faster, cheaper and produces a more consistent slab.

How many bags of concrete equal one cubic yard?

It depends on the bag size:

  • 45 bags of 80 lb — the most common size
  • 60 bags of 60 lb
  • 72 bags of 50 lb
  • 90 bags of 40 lb

The reason is yield. An 80 lb bag yields 0.60 cubic feet, and 27 × 0.60 = 16.2 cu ft per bag, which divides into 27 cu ft exactly 45 times. A 50 lb bag yields 0.375 cu ft, so you need 72.

Always check the published yield on your actual bag. Standard premix follows the numbers above, but some fast-setting and specialty mixes yield less, and a lighter-weight mix changes the count entirely.

For weight: 45 bags of 80 lb is 3,600 lb, which is exactly one cubic yard of normal-weight concrete at about 150 lb per cubic foot.

What is a short-load fee for concrete delivery?

A short-load fee is the extra charge a ready-mix plant adds when your order is less than 10 cubic yards — because the truck still has to come out, and the driver still has to wash it out afterwards.

It typically runs $60 to $200 on top of the base delivery charge of $80 to $180. For most plants a single yard is the practical minimum.

This fee is the single most important number in the bags-versus-ready-mix comparison. A one-yard order with a $120 short-load fee and $80 delivery comes to $200 in access charges alone — enough to eat the entire saving on the concrete itself.

Two ways to avoid it: order at least 10 yards if you genuinely need that much concrete, or keep the pour small and use bags. A third option is to schedule a shared load with another job in your area, which some plants offer specifically for small residential pours.

What concrete mix design should I order?

Match the mix to the exposure and the load.

  • 2,500 PSI — interior work, footings, fill, and anything that will be covered or protected. Not for any exterior flat work in a freeze-thaw climate.
  • 3,000 PSI — the standard for interior slabs and warm, dry climates.
  • 3,500 PSI — the most common residential exterior mix. Good default for patios and pads.
  • 4,000 PSI with air entrainment — driveways, garage floors, and anywhere the slab will see freeze-thaw cycles. Air entrainment is what makes the concrete survive frost; it is not optional in a cold climate.
  • 4,000 PSI or higher — warehouse floors, machine pads, and commercial work.

For bagged mix you will see the same numbers on the bag. Add fiber mesh for crack control in a slab with no rebar — it is not a substitute for reinforcement, but it reduces plastic shrinkage cracking in the first 24 hours.

How long can you hand-mix concrete before it is unusable?

Bagged concrete has a working time of roughly 30 to 45 minutes after mixing. Once it starts to stiffen you cannot add water to make it workable again without weakening it.

That constraint is what makes a bagged pour a planning problem rather than a shopping problem. At 62 bags for a 10 x 10 slab, you need roughly five to six hours of mixing and placing — longer than one working time. The realistic approach is to mix in small batches and place each batch immediately, which means working continuously.

If you do have to add water to keep up, add it sparingly and mix it in completely. Adding a half gallon of water per bag reduces the compressive strength by roughly 300 psi and raises the shrinkage cracking risk. Never add more than about half a gallon per bag, and never add water after the concrete has begun to set.

Cold joints — where two batches meet — are the visible result. They are permanent and they are the reason a mixer truck costs what it costs.

Are bagged and ready-mix concrete the same strength?

A bag labelled 3,000 PSI and a truck load of 3,000 PSI are designed to reach the same compressive strength at 28 days — but a hand-mixed bagged pour rarely achieves it in practice.

Three things work against you. First, water control: a plant mixes to a tight water-to-cement ratio, and hand mixing relies on your judgment, which usually means more water. Second, mixing consistency: every bagged batch varies slightly, and the variation shows as color and strength differences across the finished surface. Third, incomplete consolidation: air voids left in hand-mixed concrete reduce strength and let water in.

None of this matters much for a shed pad on stable ground. It matters a great deal for a driveway, a garage floor or anything carrying a load.

A reasonable rule: use bags for anything under a cubic yard that carries no structural load, and use ready-mix for anything that will be driven on, walked on heavily, or that spans a gap.
