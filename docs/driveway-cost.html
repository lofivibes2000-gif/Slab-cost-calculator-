How much does a concrete driveway cost?

A residential concrete driveway runs $6 to $12 per square foot installed in 2026, and most projects land between $7 and $10. Driveways price above patios for three reasons: more thickness, steel reinforcement, and a deeper base.

The IRC does not regulate driveway thickness — local codes do, and published standards run from 4 to 7 inches. Read your municipality's driveway standard before you pour.

What a complete quote includes

  1. Excavation, grading, and removal of the old slab and driveway
  2. 6 inches of compacted crushed stone base
  3. Forms along both edges, plus the apron at the street
  4. #3 or #4 rebar on chairs at mid-depth, tied in both directions
  5. 4,000 PSI concrete with air entrainment in freeze-thaw climates
  6. Delivery, short-load fees, and a pump if the truck cannot reach
  7. Placing, screeding, broom finishing, and edging
  8. Saw-cut control joints on the correct grid
  9. Curing compound, and cleanup and haul-off

The apron is the expensive part. Where the driveway meets the street it is often thicker, stronger, and subject to a permit or an approved detail at the right of way. Some jurisdictions require 6 inches and #4 rebar there while the rest of your driveway is 5 inches and #3.

Resurface or replace?

The cost to resurface a driveway averages $3.60 per square foot in 2026. Whether that is the right call depends entirely on what is wrong with the slab underneath.

Demo and haul-off of an existing 4-inch slab adds roughly $4 per sq ft. Resurfacing typically adds about 25% to a full replacement cost once prep is included — so on a badly failing slab, replacement often works out cheaper than resurfacing plus the repairs you would need anyway.

How much does a concrete slab cost per square foot?

A standard residential concrete slab runs $6 to $12 per square foot installed in 2026, with most projects landing near $8 per square foot. That figure includes ready-mix concrete, delivery, a compacted gravel base, forms, reinforcement, placing and finishing labor, control joints, and curing compound — but not major excavation, permits, or decorative finishes.

The cost per square foot breaks down roughly like this:

  • Concrete material: $2–$3.30 per sq ft
  • Labor (pour and finish): $3–$8 per sq ft — usually the single biggest line
  • Site prep and forms: $1–$2.50 per sq ft
  • Reinforcement: $0.20–$2.00 per sq ft
  • Curing and sealer: $0.25–$1.00 per sq ft

Small slabs cost more per square foot than large ones because the truck delivery, crew minimum, and forms are fixed costs spread over less area. A 10 x 10 pad typically runs $8.50–$15.00 per sq ft, while a 40 x 60 driveway lands closer to $6.50–$12.50 per sq ft. Decorative work — stamped, colored, or exposed aggregate — adds $3 to $10 per sq ft on top.

One important caveat about scope. Published per-square-foot figures disagree by nearly 40%, and the reason is that they measure different things. A quote at $6 per sq ft is often scoped to exclude delivery fees, control joints, curing compound, and site prep — real costs that a complete project pays. Price the same scope before comparing two bids; a lower number is not a better deal if it is missing items.

How much does it cost to pour a concrete slab?

Pouring a concrete slab costs $4 to $14 per square foot installed, depending on thickness, reinforcement, finish, and how much site work the ground needs. A plain broom-finished 4-inch slab on a flat, accessible site sits at the low end; a thick, reinforced slab on ground that needs grading and fill sits at the high end.

Realistic totals for common sizes at $6–$12 per sq ft:

  • 10 x 10 pad (100 sq ft): $600–$1,200
  • 12 x 12 pad (144 sq ft): $864–$1,730
  • 20 x 20 slab (400 sq ft): $2,400–$4,800
  • 24 x 24 garage pad (576 sq ft): $3,500–$9,000
  • 30 x 30 slab (900 sq ft): $3,600–$7,200

A useful shortcut: the concrete itself is only about 25% of an installed slab. Labor is usually 45–55%. That is why a slab quoted at $4 per square foot for materials alone is not out of line — it simply excludes the crew.

How many bags of concrete do I need?

Divide the pour's volume in cubic feet by the finished yield of one bag, then round up. Standard yields are 0.30 ft³ for a 40 lb bag, 0.45 ft³ for a 60 lb bag, and 0.60 ft³ for an 80 lb bag.

Bags = ceil( volume in ft³ x (1 + waste%) / yield per bag )

One cubic yard of concrete equals about 90 bags of 40 lb, 60 bags of 60 lb, or 45 bags of 80 lb mix.

Worked example — a 10 x 10 ft slab at 4 inches thick:

  • Volume = 10 x 10 x (4 ÷ 12) = 33.3 ft³ = 1.23 yd³
  • With 10% waste: 36.7 ft³
  • 80 lb bags: ceil(36.7 ÷ 0.60) = 62 bags
  • 60 lb bags: ceil(36.7 ÷ 0.45) = 82 bags

Bagged mix stops making sense above roughly 1 cubic yard. Past about 45 bags of 80 lb mix, hauling, mixing, and pouring by hand costs more in time and labor than a ready-mix truck — and cold joints between batches are far more likely.

How many yards of concrete do I need?

Multiply length by width by thickness in feet, then divide by 27. There are 27 cubic feet in a cubic yard.

Cubic yards = (Length ft x Width ft x Thickness in ÷ 12) ÷ 27
Shortcut: Cubic yards = (sq ft x thickness in) ÷ 324

For a 20 x 20 ft slab at 4 inches: (400 x 4) ÷ 324 = 4.94 cubic yards. Add 5–10% for waste and order about 5.5 yards.

As a rule of thumb, one cubic yard covers about 81 sq ft at 4 inches, 65 sq ft at 5 inches, 54 sq ft at 6 inches, and 41 sq ft at 8 inches. A standard ready-mix truck carries about 10 cubic yards.

How thick should a concrete slab be?

Spec it by load, not by rule of thumb:

  • 3.5–4 in — walkways, garden paths, patios, shed and AC pads (foot traffic only)
  • 4 in — the standard residential minimum; patios and light floors
  • 5 in — residential driveways and garage floors carrying passenger cars or half-ton pickups
  • 6 in — driveways taking trucks and RVs, heavy equipment pads, and commercial slabs
  • 8 in — post-tensioned, heavily loaded, or industrial slabs

For code: the International Residential Code (R506.1) sets a 3.5-inch minimum for slab-on-grade floors in habitable space, and most jurisdictions amend that to 4 inches. The IRC does not regulate exterior patios and walkways, though local codes often do govern driveways — published driveway standards run from 4 to 7 inches depending on the municipality.

ACI 330R-08 puts it plainly: "As a practical matter, there is no benefit in building slabs less than 4 in. (100 mm) thick." Thickness is the cheapest structural upgrade available — bending capacity scales with the square of thickness, so 4 in to 6 in is 50% more concrete but roughly 2.25x the bending resistance.

What PSI concrete do I need for a slab?

Match the strength to the exposure and the load:

  • 3,000 PSI — interior slabs, basement floors, and shop floors that never see salt or freezing
  • 3,500 PSI — standard patios, sidewalks, and exterior residential work
  • 4,000 PSI — driveways, garage floors, and any exterior slab in a freeze-thaw climate, specified with 5–7% air entrainment
  • 4,500–5,000 PSI — heavy vehicle pads, warehouse slabs, and structural work per ACI 318

ACI 332 requires 4,000 PSI with air entrainment for exterior residential slabs in freeze-thaw zones. Each 500 PSI step up adds roughly $4–$10 per cubic yard, which is cheap insurance against scaling and spalling. In freeze-thaw climates, air entrainment matters more than an extra inch of thickness — 3,000 PSI slabs without air can scale badly within 5 to 10 years.

How much does a concrete driveway cost?

A residential concrete driveway costs $6 to $12 per square foot installed in 2026, and most projects land between $7 and $10.

Typical totals:

  • 10 x 20 driveway (200 sq ft): $1,200–$2,400
  • 12 x 24 driveway (288 sq ft): $1,700–$3,500
  • 12 x 40 two-car driveway (480 sq ft): $2,900–$5,800
  • 20 x 50 driveway (1,000 sq ft): $6,000–$12,000

Driveways price above patios for three reasons: they are thicker (5–6 inches rather than 4), they need rebar instead of light mesh, and the base is deeper (6 inches of compacted stone rather than 4).

The apron where the driveway meets the street is often the most expensive part of the job, because it is thicker, stronger, and sometimes a permit is involved — some jurisdictions require an approved detail at the right of way.

How much does a longer driveway cost per square foot?

Longer driveways cost less per square foot, because delivery, crew setup, and mobilization are spread over more area.

A 12 x 20 driveway (240 sq ft) may land near $8.50–$15.00 per sq ft. The same crew pouring a 12 x 60 driveway (720 sq ft) typically lands nearer $6.50–$12.50 per sq ft. The absolute total goes up; the rate per square foot goes down.

This is the same effect that makes small slabs expensive. A short driveway also carries proportionally more cost for the truck trip, forms, and the crew minimum — there is a practical minimum charge for any concrete pour, and a 150 sq ft job can trip it.

If you are comparing quotes, compare the per-square-foot rate on the quote, not just the total. A low total on a small job can still be a high rate.

Should I resurface my concrete driveway or replace it?

Resurface when the slab is sound but the surface is worn; replace when the slab itself is failing.

Resurfacing makes sense when the concrete underneath is solid, the cracks are hairline, and the problem is surface spalling, scaling, or discoloration. A 1 to 2 inch overlay costs about $3.60 per square foot, roughly $2,160 for a 600 sq ft driveway. It adds about 25% to the total project cost including prep, and it extends the life of a sound slab by several years.

Replace when you have deep cracking, heaving or settling, spalling that goes through the full depth, or a base failure. Removing and hauling off an old 4-inch slab runs about $4 per square foot, and a full replacement often costs less than a resurface plus the repairs you would need anyway.

The cost of resurfacing a driveway runs about $3.60 per square foot in 2026, with most jobs landing between $2,200 and $6,200 depending on size, prep, and whether the old slab is removed first. Get a structural opinion before resurfacing over a failing slab — a new surface over a bad base fails too, and sooner.

How long after pouring can I walk or drive on a concrete slab?

Concrete gains most of its strength in the first week, but design strength is measured at 28 days.

  • 24–48 hours — keep foot traffic off. Walking is usually fine after 24–48 hours.
  • 7 days — typical for passenger vehicles. Some municipalities allow traffic after 7 days when testing confirms 2,500 psi.
  • 28 days — full specified strength, the point at which you can park heavy equipment, back a loaded trailer, or place a hot tub.

Keep the slab damp during the first 3 to 7 days. Apply curing compound as soon as the surface water is gone — it slows evaporation and keeps the hydration reaction going. Curing is not a formality: a slab that dries out too early loses surface strength, and that is where you see the dusty, flaky top that shows up six months later.

How thick should a concrete driveway be?

5 to 6 inches for a driveway carrying passenger vehicles. That is the standard, and it is thicker than a patio for a reason: a vehicle load is far higher than a foot traffic load, and a thin slab over a soft base deflects and cracks across its span.

  • 4 in — walkways and garden paths only
  • 5 in — passenger car driveways and garage floors
  • 6 in — heavy vehicles, RVs, dumpsters, and any slab over fill
  • 8 in — post-tensioned, commercial, or engineered loads

Going from 5 to 6 inches is about $240 more of concrete on a 288 sq ft driveway — cheap insurance. Going the wrong way and pouring 4 inches on a driveway is the expensive mistake, because a replacement means breaking it out.

The thickness only performs if the base is compacted and the slab is reinforced. See the rebar guidance for spacing.

Do I need rebar in a concrete driveway?

Yes. A driveway is the most common residential slab where reinforcement is genuinely required, because it spans, it carries concentrated vehicle loads, and it is often poured over disturbed fill.

The standard detail is #4 rebar at 18 inches on center, both ways, for a 5 to 6 inch slab. For a 6 inch slab carrying heavier loads, step up to #5 at 12 inches on center.

Reinforcement does not stop a slab from cracking — a slab will crack. What it does is hold the crack together so the two halves stay interlocked and the surface stays level and watertight. Without it, a crack opens into a gap that collects water and ravel.

Two practical points. Rebar goes in the bottom third of the slab on chairs, with at least 2 inches of cover from the edge. And a driveway needs no extra joint beyond the control joints — a longitudinal joint down the middle of a 12-foot-wide drive is not required.

How much does it cost to remove and replace a concrete driveway?

Tearing out and replacing an existing driveway costs roughly 15 to 30% more than pouring a new one — about $2,000 to $3,200 for a 12 x 24 ft two-car driveway in 2026.

The added cost is demolition: sawing the slab into liftable pieces, breaking it out, hauling it away and disposing of it runs $6 to $12 per square foot of slab. On 288 sq ft that is $1,700 to $3,500 on its own.

Two ways to cut it. First, only replace what has failed — saw at the control joints and pour new sections, keeping the sound concrete. Second, overlay: a 3 to 4 inch bonded concrete surface over an existing sound slab costs roughly 60% of a full replacement, and a 1.5 inch stamped overlay on top of that is less again.

An overlay is only viable if the existing slab is sound, level and free of active cracks. If it is heaving or cracking through, it has to come out.

How long after pouring a driveway can I drive on it?

Wait 7 days before driving on a new concrete driveway, and 24 to 48 hours before walking on it.

Concrete reaches most of its strength by 28 days, but it gains enough strength to carry a passenger car at around 7 days. A full pickup or an RV loaded with several thousand pounds should wait the full 28 days.

The fastest way to damage a new driveway is to park on it early. Concrete cures by hydrating — it is gaining strength from moisture, not drying. Loading it before it has bonded means tire marks that can be permanent, and in cold weather an early load can cause a structural crack.

During the cure, keep the curing compound on and, if you used one, leave the plastic sheeting down. Do not seal or apply any other product to the surface for at least 28 days — sealers applied early can trap moisture and cause surface scaling.

Where do control joints go in a concrete driveway?

Control joints divide a slab into panels small enough that any cracking happens at the joint, where it is filled and sealed, instead of randomly across the surface.

The rule is simple: space them no more than 10 feet apart for a 4 inch slab, and no more than 12 feet for a 5 to 6 inch driveway. Divide the length by 10 and you have your panel count.

A 24-foot driveway wants three sections, jointed at about 8 feet. Joints run across the driveway, not down the middle — a longitudinal joint in the center of a drive is not needed and adds an extra line to seal.

Two ways to form a joint. Saw cutting is the common method: cut within 1 to 4 hours of finishing, to a depth of one quarter of the slab thickness. Grooving with a hand tool during finishing is faster but only works on a straight edge. Either way, fill the joint with a flexible sealer — it is the joint that fails, not the concrete.

Do I need to seal a concrete driveway?

Not immediately, and not strictly at all. Sealing is optional on a properly placed, well-jointed driveway. What is not optional is keeping the joints sealed — that is what stops water reaching the rebar and the base.

If you do seal, wait 28 days after the pour. Apply a penetrating sealer rather than an acrylic film: penetrating sealers soak into the surface and leave the natural texture, while acrylics sit on top and can peel or yellow.

The best timing depends on your climate. In a freeze-thaw region, seal before the first freeze. In a hot, dry region, sealing the first year actually helps — concrete that has not fully cured loses moisture faster than it can replace it, and a sealer slows that down.

Plan to renew every 3 to 5 years. And apply it to both the slab and the joints — a sealed slab with an unsealed joint still lets water in at the worst possible place.
