Reinforcement guide

What size rebar, and how far apart?

Reinforcement is what stops a slab from cracking along its length as the ground moves and the concrete shrinks. Pick the row that matches your slab thickness and what the slab will carry.

Slab thickness Typical use Bar size Spacing (on center) Lap / overlap
3–4 in Patio, shed pad, AC pad, walkway #3 (3/8 in) 24 in on center 12 in
4 in Residential floor, garage floor #4 (1/2 in) 18 in on center 16 in
5–6 in Driveway, heavy vehicle, RV pad #4–#5 12 in on center 16–20 in
8 in+ Commercial, post-tensioned, heavy loads #5–#6 6–12 in on center 20–24 in

Lap splice = 40 × bar diameter, so 12 in for #3, 16 in for #4 and 20 in for #5. Reinforcement goes in the bottom third of the slab with at least 2 in of cover measured from the slab edge — 1.5 in is the IRC minimum for #5 and smaller in residential slabs. Where the ground is stable and the load is light, welded wire mesh at 6 in by 6 in is a cheaper alternative, but it is easier to walk out of place than rebar.

Rebar is not required for a slab on well-compacted, stable soil with light loads — many patios and shed pads go unreinforced with a fiber mesh or a thickened edge. It is required where the slab spans, carries vehicles, or sits on fill. Check the local code before you skip it.

The three questions everyone asks first

How many bags of concrete do I need?

Divide the pour's volume in cubic feet by the bag yield, then round up: 0.30 ft³ for a 40 lb bag, 0.375 ft³ for 50 lb, 0.45 ft³ for 60 lb, 0.60 ft³ for 80 lb.

A 10 × 10 ft slab at 4 inches takes 1.23 yd³ — which is 62 bags of 80 lb, or 82 of 60 lb, once you add 10% for waste.

How many yards of concrete do I need?

Length × width × thickness (in feet) ÷ 27. Or use the shortcut: (square feet × thickness in inches) ÷ 324.

A 20 × 20 ft slab at 4 inches is (400 × 4) ÷ 324 = 4.94 cubic yards. Order about 5.5 with waste.

A 20 × 20 slab with base, forms, mesh, labor, joints, and curing lands around $3,200–$4,400 depending on scope.

How much does a concrete slab cost per square foot?

A standard residential concrete slab runs $6 to $12 per square foot installed in 2026, with most projects landing near $8 per square foot. That figure includes ready-mix concrete, delivery, a compacted gravel base, forms, reinforcement, placing and finishing labor, control joints, and curing compound — but not major excavation, permits, or decorative finishes.

The cost per square foot breaks down roughly like this:

  • Concrete material: $2–$3.30 per sq ft
  • Labor (pour and finish): $3–$8 per sq ft — usually the single biggest line
  • Site prep and forms: $1–$2.50 per sq ft
  • Reinforcement: $0.20–$2.00 per sq ft
  • Curing and sealer: $0.25–$1.00 per sq ft

Small slabs cost more per square foot than large ones because the truck delivery, crew minimum, and forms are fixed costs spread over less area. A 10 x 10 pad typically runs $8.50–$15.00 per sq ft, while a 40 x 60 driveway lands closer to $6.50–$12.50 per sq ft. Decorative work — stamped, colored, or exposed aggregate — adds $3 to $10 per sq ft on top.

One important caveat about scope. Published per-square-foot figures disagree by nearly 40%, and the reason is that they measure different things. A quote at $6 per sq ft is often scoped to exclude delivery fees, control joints, curing compound, and site prep — real costs that a complete project pays. Price the same scope before comparing two bids; a lower number is not a better deal if it is missing items.

How much does it cost to pour a concrete slab?

Pouring a concrete slab costs $4 to $14 per square foot installed, depending on thickness, reinforcement, finish, and how much site work the ground needs. A plain broom-finished 4-inch slab on a flat, accessible site sits at the low end; a thick, reinforced slab on ground that needs grading and fill sits at the high end.

Realistic totals for common sizes at $6–$12 per sq ft:

  • 10 x 10 pad (100 sq ft): $600–$1,200
  • 12 x 12 pad (144 sq ft): $864–$1,730
  • 20 x 20 slab (400 sq ft): $2,400–$4,800
  • 24 x 24 garage pad (576 sq ft): $3,500–$9,000
  • 30 x 30 slab (900 sq ft): $3,600–$7,200

A useful shortcut: the concrete itself is only about 25% of an installed slab. Labor is usually 45–55%. That is why a slab quoted at $4 per square foot for materials alone is not out of line — it simply excludes the crew.

How many bags of concrete do I need?

Divide the pour's volume in cubic feet by the finished yield of one bag, then round up. Standard yields are 0.30 ft³ for a 40 lb bag, 0.45 ft³ for a 60 lb bag, and 0.60 ft³ for an 80 lb bag.

Bags = ceil( volume in ft³ x (1 + waste%) / yield per bag )

One cubic yard of concrete equals about 90 bags of 40 lb, 60 bags of 60 lb, or 45 bags of 80 lb mix.

Worked example — a 10 x 10 ft slab at 4 inches thick:

  • Volume = 10 x 10 x (4 ÷ 12) = 33.3 ft³ = 1.23 yd³
  • With 10% waste: 36.7 ft³
  • 80 lb bags: ceil(36.7 ÷ 0.60) = 62 bags
  • 60 lb bags: ceil(36.7 ÷ 0.45) = 82 bags

Bagged mix stops making sense above roughly 1 cubic yard. Past about 45 bags of 80 lb mix, hauling, mixing, and pouring by hand costs more in time and labor than a ready-mix truck — and cold joints between batches are far more likely.

How many yards of concrete do I need?

Multiply length by width by thickness in feet, then divide by 27. There are 27 cubic feet in a cubic yard.

Cubic yards = (Length ft x Width ft x Thickness in ÷ 12) ÷ 27
Shortcut: Cubic yards = (sq ft x thickness in) ÷ 324

For a 20 x 20 ft slab at 4 inches: (400 x 4) ÷ 324 = 4.94 cubic yards. Add 5–10% for waste and order about 5.5 yards.

As a rule of thumb, one cubic yard covers about 81 sq ft at 4 inches, 65 sq ft at 5 inches, 54 sq ft at 6 inches, and 41 sq ft at 8 inches. A standard ready-mix truck carries about 10 cubic yards.

How thick should a concrete slab be?

Spec it by load, not by rule of thumb:

  • 3.5–4 in — walkways, garden paths, patios, shed and AC pads (foot traffic only)
  • 4 in — the standard residential minimum; patios and light floors
  • 5 in — residential driveways and garage floors carrying passenger cars or half-ton pickups
  • 6 in — driveways taking trucks and RVs, heavy equipment pads, and commercial slabs
  • 8 in — post-tensioned, heavily loaded, or industrial slabs

For code: the International Residential Code (R506.1) sets a 3.5-inch minimum for slab-on-grade floors in habitable space, and most jurisdictions amend that to 4 inches. The IRC does not regulate exterior patios and walkways, though local codes often do govern driveways — published driveway standards run from 4 to 7 inches depending on the municipality.

ACI 330R-08 puts it plainly: "As a practical matter, there is no benefit in building slabs less than 4 in. (100 mm) thick." Thickness is the cheapest structural upgrade available — bending capacity scales with the square of thickness, so 4 in to 6 in is 50% more concrete but roughly 2.25x the bending resistance.

What PSI concrete do I need for a slab?

Match the strength to the exposure and the load:

  • 3,000 PSI — interior slabs, basement floors, and shop floors that never see salt or freezing
  • 3,500 PSI — standard patios, sidewalks, and exterior residential work
  • 4,000 PSI — driveways, garage floors, and any exterior slab in a freeze-thaw climate, specified with 5–7% air entrainment
  • 4,500–5,000 PSI — heavy vehicle pads, warehouse slabs, and structural work per ACI 318

ACI 332 requires 4,000 PSI with air entrainment for exterior residential slabs in freeze-thaw zones. Each 500 PSI step up adds roughly $4–$10 per cubic yard, which is cheap insurance against scaling and spalling. In freeze-thaw climates, air entrainment matters more than an extra inch of thickness — 3,000 PSI slabs without air can scale badly within 5 to 10 years.

How do I calculate the amount of concrete I need?

Multiply the three dimensions of the pour together, then convert to the unit you will buy in.

Cubic feet = Length (ft) x Width (ft) x Thickness (ft)
Cubic yards = Cubic feet ÷ 27
Cubic yards = (sq ft x thickness in) ÷ 324

The 324 shortcut combines the 12-inch-to-foot conversion and the 27 cubic feet in a cubic yard into one step. For a 20 x 20 ft slab at 4 inches: (400 x 4) ÷ 324 = 4.94 cubic yards.

Then add 5 to 10% for waste — spillage, uneven subgrade, over-excavation, and the mix left in the chute. NRMCA recommends ordering at least 4 to 10% more than the plan volume. Round up to the next quarter yard.

For shapes other than a rectangle, split the pour into rectangles and add the results.

How many cubic feet is a 50 lb bag of concrete?

A 50 lb bag of premixed concrete yields about 0.375 cubic feet — roughly 72 bags per cubic yard.

Full yield table for standard premix bag sizes:

  • 40 lb — 0.30 ft³ per bag · 90 bags per yd³
  • 50 lb — 0.375 ft³ per bag · 72 bags per yd³
  • 60 lb — 0.45 ft³ per bag · 60 bags per yd³
  • 80 lb — 0.60 ft³ per bag · 45 bags per yd³

The largest bag is always the best value per cubic foot — an 80 lb bag gives you 0.60 ft³ of concrete for fewer trips than two 40 lb bags, and it is the size most hardware stores stock in pallet quantity.

Check the yield on your actual bag. These are the standard published yields, but some brands and fast-setting mixes yield less. The spec sheet on the bag is authoritative.

Should I order concrete by the yard from a ready mix company or just use bags?

Use bags below about 1 cubic yard — roughly 45 bags of 80 lb mix. Use ready-mix above that.

The practical differences:

  • Cost per cubic yard is nearly identical. A yard of ready-mix at $155 runs about $155; the same yard in 80 lb bags at $6.50 runs about $292. But bags avoid the delivery trip fee and the short-load surcharge that make a small ready-mix order expensive.
  • Time is the real difference. One cubic yard of finished concrete weighs 4,050 lb. Hand-mixing it is a full day for two people plus a mixer rental. A ready-mix pour is about an hour.
  • Cold joints. Hand-mixed pours that take more than a couple of hours often show visible joints where the water content changed between batches.
  • Consistency. Every yard off a truck has the same water-to-cement ratio. Every hand-mixed batch is a guess.

The exception is a small pour in a backyard the truck cannot reach. If delivery means a pump truck or a long wheelbarrow haul, bags can still win on total cost even past a cubic yard.

What's the difference between an online calculator and a physical construction calculator?

A physical construction calculator (the circular disc-and-window type you hang on a belt) does arithmetic fast — it multiplies, divides, and runs trig in your head. It does not know anything about concrete.

An online concrete calculator carries the domain knowledge instead:

  • It knows that one cubic yard is 27 cubic feet, and that an 80 lb bag yields 0.60 ft³
  • It knows the 324 shortcut, the 81 sq ft coverage rule, and the standard waste allowance
  • It knows the thickness, PSI, reinforcement, and joint-spacing conventions
  • It carries current regional pricing and can compare ready-mix against bagged mix

They are complementary rather than competing. The physical calculator is still faster for mental arithmetic on site — "what is 400 times 4 over 324?" — and the online tool is what tells you which numbers to multiply and what the answer means for your order.

How much concrete do I need for a footing or a column?

The formula is the same, only the shape changes.

Strip footing (a long narrow pour): Volume = length x width x depth

A 10 ft x 2 ft footing at 12 inches deep is 20 cu ft — about 0.74 cubic yards.

Round column or sonotube: Volume = pi x radius² x height

A 12 inch diameter column at 4 feet tall is 3.14 x 1 x 4 = 12.6 cu ft — about 0.46 cubic yards.

Two things people forget on columns: add half an inch per side if you are setting the tube in place rather than pouring against a form, and remember a column's weight is what determines the base and footing size — a 12 inch column weighs about 300 lb per foot of height.

What size bag of concrete should I buy?

Match the bag to the volume of the job, and match the strength to the job.

  • 40 lb — 0.30 ft³ yield. Best for small patches and footings.
  • 50 lb — 0.375 ft³ yield. A middle option when 60 and 80 lb are too heavy to handle.
  • 60 lb — 0.45 ft³ yield. A good middle option for DIY work.
  • 80 lb — 0.60 ft³ yield. The most common size; heaviest to handle, best value per bag.

For strength, choose 3,000 PSI for interior or sheltered work, 3,500 PSI for standard exterior residential, and 4,000 PSI with air entrainment for driveways, garage floors, and freeze-thaw climates.

Do not mix bag sizes within one pour. Different mixes and different water contents will show up as visible color and strength differences in the finished surface.

How do I calculate concrete for a non-rectangular shape?

Break the shape into rectangles, calculate each one, then add the volumes together. It is the same arithmetic every time.

L-shaped slab — split it into a full-length rectangle plus a second rectangle covering the notch:

  • Part A = length x width
  • Part B = (length − cutout length) x cutout width
  • Total area = A + B

Circular pad — area = π x (diameter ÷ 2)²

Footing — length x width x depth, and do not forget to add the depth of the footing that sits below the grade line.

Columns — π x radius² x height. Add a 1/2 inch allowance per side for the forms.

Walls — length x thickness x height.

Stairs — width x (run ÷ 2 x rise) for the solid flight, plus the landing block.

How do I convert between cubic feet, cubic yards, and bags?

Three conversions cover almost everything:

  • 1 cubic yard = 27 cubic feet
  • 1 cubic yard = 90 bags of 40 lb, 72 of 50 lb, 60 of 60 lb, or 45 of 80 lb
  • 1 cubic yard covers 324 ÷ thickness(inches) square feet

So 1 cubic yard spreads over 81 sq ft at 4 inches, 65 sq ft at 5 inches, 54 sq ft at 6 inches, and 41 sq ft at 8 inches.

To convert bag counts to weight, multiply bags by bag size — 45 bags of 80 lb is 3,600 lb, which is the weight of one cubic yard of normal-weight concrete. Around 150 lb per cubic foot is the standard density for structural concrete; lightweight mixes run 90–120 lb per cubic foot, and you must check the bag's published yield for anything non-standard.

What is the standard thickness of concrete?

There is no single standard, because thickness follows load. The residential standard is 4 inches, and it covers most work:

  • 3.5–4 in — walkways, garden paths, and light pads
  • 4 in — patios, shed pads, AC pads, and residential floors (the common standard)
  • 5 in — driveways and garage floors taking passenger cars
  • 6 in — heavy vehicle driveways, RV and boat pads, commercial slabs
  • 8 in — post-tensioned, heavily loaded, or industrial slabs

Two anchors on this. The IRC (R506.1) sets a 3.5-inch minimum for slab-on-grade floors in habitable space, and most jurisdictions amend that to 4 inches. And ACI 330R-08 puts it bluntly: "As a practical matter, there is no benefit in building slabs less than 4 in. (100 mm) thick."

Thickness is the cheapest structural upgrade available — bending capacity scales with the square of thickness, so going from 4 to 6 inches is 50% more concrete but roughly 2.25× the bending resistance.

How many cubic yards of concrete do I need?

Divide the plan area by the coverage rate for your slab thickness. One cubic yard of concrete covers:

  • 81 sq ft at 4 inches thick
  • 65 sq ft at 5 inches
  • 54 sq ft at 6 inches
  • 41 sq ft at 8 inches

So a 400 sq ft slab at 4 inches needs 400 ÷ 81 = 4.94 cubic yards. The same calculation in one step, using thickness in inches, is:

Cubic yards = (square feet × thickness in inches) ÷ 324

Then add 5 to 10% for waste — spillage, uneven subgrade and the mix left in the chute — and round up to the next quarter yard. A 400 sq ft slab at 4 inches comes to 5.43 cubic yards ordered. Ready-mix plants sell in quarter-yard and half-yard increments, so ask for 5.5.

How do I calculate concrete in metric units?

Switch the calculator to metric and it handles the conversion for you, but the underlying shortcut is worth knowing.

Cubic metres = length (m) × width (m) × thickness (m)
Cubic yards = cubic metres × 1.308

One cubic metre of concrete covers 13.1 square metres at 10 cm thick, 10.5 m² at 12.5 cm and 8.7 m² at 15 cm.

For example, a 6.1 m × 6.1 m slab at 10 cm: 6.1 × 6.1 × 0.10 = 3.72 m³, which is 4.87 cubic yards — the same answer the imperial inputs give.

Bag counts work the same way. One cubic metre needs 54 bags of 80 lb mix, 62 bags of 60 lb, 75 bags of 50 lb, or 90 bags of 40 lb. Metric and imperial are the same pour — only the labels change.

How much concrete do I need for a triangular slab?

A triangle's plan area is simply base × height ÷ 2, then multiply by thickness for volume.

Area (sq ft) = base (ft) × height (ft) ÷ 2
Volume (cu ft) = area × thickness (ft)

A triangular pad with a 20 ft base and a 12 ft height gives 20 × 12 ÷ 2 = 120 sq ft of plan area. At 4 inches thick that is 120 × 0.333 = 40 cu ft, or 1.48 cubic yards — about 73 bags of 80 lb mix with waste.

The perimeter differs by triangle type. A symmetric triangle with the apex centred over the base needs 20 + 2 × √(10² + 12²) = 48.3 ft of edge. A right triangle with the 90° corner at one end needs 20 + 12 + √(20² + 12²) = 52.4 ft. That edge length drives your formwork cost.

The calculator has a Triangle mode with both options, and a Custom mode if the triangle is only part of the pour.

How do I calculate concrete for several separate areas on one site?

Calculate each area separately and add the volumes together. Every shape has its own formula, but the arithmetic after that is simple addition.

A common case: a 20 x 20 ft garage pad plus a 10 x 10 ft stoop. The garage is 400 sq ft and the stoop is 100 sq ft, so 500 sq ft total. At 4 inches that is 500 × 0.333 = 166.7 cu ft = 6.17 cubic yards before waste, or 6.79 with a 10% allowance.

The Custom mode in this calculator does exactly this. Add a rectangle, a round pad and a triangle, enter each one's dimensions, and the area, volume and total edge length are summed live. Shared edges between two areas are counted twice in the edge length, because each area still needs its own form unless the two are cast in a single continuous pour.

One caution: separate pours on the same site should still be tied together with rebar or a construction joint, and each pour needs its own control joints.

How much gravel do I need under a concrete slab?

A 4-inch compacted crushed stone base is the standard under a residential slab, and the volume follows the same shortcut as the concrete.

Gravel (yd³) = (square feet × base depth in inches) ÷ 324

For a 400 sq ft slab at 4 inches: (400 × 4) ÷ 324 = 1.48 cubic yards. At 6 inches it becomes 2.22 cubic yards. Round up and order roughly half a yard extra — the base gets raked and compacted, and material is lost at the edges.

Two things to get right. First, the gravel must be compacted — a plate compactor or a rented roller, in two or three passes. Concrete poured over loose fill will crack, and no amount of rebar fixes a settling sub-base. Second, the base is what lets water drain out; the vapor barrier goes on top of the compacted base, then the rebar, then the concrete.

The calculator's Gravel base tile updates the volume whenever you change the base depth.

How much waste should I add to my concrete order?

Order 5 to 10% more than the calculated volume. NRMCA's guidance is 4 to 10% for any slab with irregular edges, and most contractors order at the upper end.

Where the waste comes from: spillage during the pour, uneven subgrade, over-excavation, the mix left in the truck chute, and the fact that a finished slab's true edges rarely match the tape measure exactly.

Set the waste factor to:

  • 0–5% for a square, well-measured slab with straight formwork
  • 10% for most residential work — the standard default
  • 15–20% for irregular shapes, hand-packed forms, or a long access haul where the wheelbarrow loses material

Never plan to order the bare calculated volume. Under-ordering means a cold joint in the middle of a slab, which is the single most visible concrete defect there is.
